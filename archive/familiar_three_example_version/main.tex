\title{Choosing familiar charts by task-specific optimization}
\author{Mohamed Elgendi\\Khalifa University, Abu Dhabi, United Arab Emirates}
\date{}
\begin{document}\maketitle
\begin{abstract}
Which familiar chart best preserves the information needed for a particular comparison? We present an executable procedure that renders candidate charts, recovers a declared numerical target from their pixels and ranks the resulting errors on an interpretable score. The main analysis compares seven familiar chart families across 21 settings for 127 two-group comparisons from nine public resources. For a common task describing group differences from low to high values, the best family varies: probability heatmaps win 54 comparisons, dot plots 39, histograms 18, violins 13 and mean bars three. Worked examples show how the score is calculated, when changing the chart improves recovery and when the starting chart should be retained. Scores measure computational recovery, not human comprehension, and mean bars can score well without preserving full distributions. Supplementary task-matched controls often outperform the familiar set, demonstrating that the optimum depends on the declared candidates as well as the data, question and observer. These retrospective results support reproducible, conditional chart selection; they do not establish a universally best chart or improved human reading accuracy.
\end{abstract}

\section*{Introduction}
A chart should be evaluated against the question its reader is expected to answer. Comparing two means, comparing distributional shapes and estimating individual change are different tasks. Displays that omit the required information cannot become adequate simply through higher resolution. Conversely, retaining observations does not establish that a reader can recover the relevant information accurately. Distributional comparisons and shift functions have long provided alternatives to mean-only summaries.\cite{weissgerber2015,rousselet2017} Scientific visualization requires explicit objectives and evidence that the resulting displays support those objectives.\cite{elgendi2017,elgendi2020}

Automated graphical design, constraint-based systems and learned recommenders address chart construction and recommendation.\cite{mackinlay1986,draco,vizml} Task taxonomies clarify the analytic operations a visualization should support, while graphical-perception experiments evaluate human judgments.\cite{amar2005,cleveland1984} These contributions motivate, but do not validate, a separate computational question: given known data and a specified recovery algorithm, which rendering most accurately communicates a declared numerical target?

A score alone does not answer whether the recommended chart is useful. A color-calibrated decoder may recover faint heatmap intensities more accurately than a person. A mean-only baseline can be predictably weak for a distributional task. Searching more settings in one family can increase its opportunity to win. Nearly identical scores may exchange rank with a small change in resolution. These concerns require explicit controls rather than a transformation that forces every chart to have a unique score.

We study a practical optimization problem: given two samples, a stated question and a declared set of familiar chart families, which family and setting best preserve the requested information under a reproducible computational reading rule? The optimizer chooses a chart for each comparison rather than naming one family as a universal winner. The main analysis uses seven familiar families and 21 settings. ``Familiar'' describes this project's chosen set; it is not a measured worldwide popularity ranking. Line, scatter and pie charts address other data structures or questions and are not assigned artificial scores for this marginal distribution task.

The main figures follow the reader's workflow. Figure~\ref{fig:scoring} demonstrates the arithmetic using a dot plot; Fig.~\ref{fig:improvement} shows different datasets selecting a violin and a histogram; and Fig.~\ref{fig:limits} shows an unchanged mean-bar chart alongside the complete winner distribution. These examples were chosen retrospectively for explanation. Supplementary analyses expand the candidates to specialized task-matched controls, assess six related tasks and test transfer to another display condition. These checks delimit the main conditional claim rather than establish a universal chart recommender.

\section*{Results}
\subsection*{A declared task connects the image to the score}
For samples $x$ and $y$, the evaluator computes
\begin{equation}
\Delta_k=Q_y(p_k)-Q_x(p_k),\quad p_k\in\{0.10,0.25,0.50,0.75,0.90\}.
\label{eq:target}
\end{equation}
These five equally weighted differences describe the lower, middle and upper central portions of two marginal distributions. They do not estimate diagnostic accuracy, a causal treatment effect or within-person change. The empirical quantiles use linear interpolation.

Let $c$ identify a chart family and setting, and $e$ a resolution--observer environment. The renderer produces an image $I_{c,e,r}$ at placement $r$. The decoder receives only that image and public calibration metadata $A_c$, including chart type, panel bounds and measurement limits. It returns estimated group quantiles $\widetilde Q_{g,c,e,r}(p_k)$. Observations, target quantiles and stored summary values are unavailable to the decoder. Define
\begin{align}
L_{c,e,r}&=\frac{1}{5s}\sum_{k=1}^5|\widetilde Q_{y,c,e,r}(p_k)-\widetilde Q_{x,c,e,r}(p_k)-\Delta_k|,\\
\overline L_{c,e}&=\frac14\sum_{r=1}^4L_{c,e,r},\qquad S_{c,e}=\frac{100}{1+\overline L_{c,e}},\label{eq:score}\\
s^2&=\frac{(n_x-1)s_x^2+(n_y-1)s_y^2}{n_x+n_y-2}.
\end{align}
All candidates for a comparison share the same pooled within-group scale $s$. A recorded one-source-unit fallback is retained for zero scale. This special case is not unit invariant. A score of 80 means an average absolute contrast error of $0.25s$, not 80\% correct readers or retained information.

Since $dS/dL=-100/(1+L)^2<0$, maximizing $S$ is exactly equivalent to minimizing the declared loss. The reciprocal transformation adds interpretability, not information or mathematical novelty. Equal losses remain equal after any strictly monotone transformation. We retain computational ties within absolute loss tolerance $10^{-12}$ and report winning margins rather than manufacturing uniqueness.

Each of the seven displayed family scores is its best valid setting. The figure gives the actual selected raster, true pulse-minus-ECG gaps, computational reading errors and the arithmetic producing the final score. ``Data spread'' denotes the pooled within-group standard deviation $s$. Define the question, evaluate the declared settings and retain the best or near-best choices; no family is prescribed in advance.

\subsection*{Information eligibility precedes numerical ranking}
Let $E_c(D)$ denote the complete image and public calibration supplied by a chart for data $D$, and let $T(D)$ denote the requested target. A necessary condition for exact recovery is
\[
 E_c(D_1)=E_c(D_2)\ \Longrightarrow\ T(D_1)=T(D_2).
\]
If two inputs have identical encoded information but different targets, any decoder must give the same answer to both. For a scalar target under absolute loss, at least one of its two errors is therefore at least $|T(D_1)-T(D_2)|/2$. This elementary observation separates missing information from imperfect pixel extraction. Lossy summaries such as mean bars remain diagnostic negative controls: an occasional win on a near-zero target does not make them sufficient distributional representations.

For example (Supplementary Fig.~S3), take paired observations $x=(0,1,2)$. Pairing them with $y=(0,1,2)$ produces median change zero; pairing them with $y=(1,2,0)$ produces median change one. The marginal distributions, common axes and marginal chart inputs are identical. A chart that retains only those marginals cannot identify the median within-person change, regardless of its resolution or decoder. We therefore reject that task for the implemented marginal representations rather than report a misleading score. Means of paired changes are a different case: their average equals the difference of marginal means. The restriction concerns targets that actually depend on pairing.

\subsection*{Two datasets demonstrate different optimal displays}
For maximum heart rate in the Cleveland resource, mean bars score 85.67 and the best familiar candidate, a violin plot with 64 density grid points, scores 98.01: a gain of 12.34 points. For blood creatinine phosphokinase in the heart-failure resource, mean bars score 85.41 and an eight-bin histogram scores 97.49: a gain of 12.09 points (Fig.~\ref{fig:improvement}). Both examples use the same five-gap question. Different choices therefore arise from the data within a common optimization problem, not from changing the target between panels. The larger scores indicate smaller computational recovery errors, not measured gains in student understanding.

\subsection*{The best choice can be the original chart}
For early versus late exercise heart rate in the wrist resource (feature 00), mean bars already achieve the best familiar-candidate score, 95.35. Selection retains them, producing a change of zero points (Fig.~\ref{fig:limits}). This example uses the same five-gap target as the preceding figures. A mean-only chart can approximate the requested contrasts on a particular sample while omitting distributional information in general. The result justifies retaining that chart for this computed objective, not declaring mean bars universally sufficient. The wrist comparison summarizes eight participants and supports no population or clinical inference here.

\subsection*{Specialized controls define the boundary of the claim}
The familiar-set restriction is an application scope, not evidence that other representations are inferior. An expanded supplementary analysis adds an empirical cumulative distribution function and a direct quantile-marker display. The latter belongs to the winning set in 122 of 127 comparisons when all 23 settings are allowed. Thus the main result is explicitly the best \emph{among the declared familiar candidates}, not the unrestricted best visualization. These specialized controls and all their scores remain in the Supplement and reproducibility files.

A further ten-configuration extension evaluates six related distributional tasks, including a 19-marker control, using resource-disjoint defaults and transfer from 256 to 384 pixels with new placements (Supplementary Sections S13--S15). Case-specific selection does not consistently improve on a strong task-matched default. These expanded-candidate findings are not presented as transfer validation of the restricted seven-family selector. They demonstrate why data-dependent reference winners alone cannot establish incremental value at a new display condition. Supplementary Figs.~S3--S6 retain the eligibility, task, transfer and encoding-error diagnostics.

\subsection*{Group fidelity and trivial baselines qualify a high score}
Contrast recovery can hide errors in both groups. We therefore report, without combining it into a new weighted score,
\begin{equation}
G_{c,e}=\frac{1}{4}\sum_r\frac{1}{10s}\sum_{g\in\{x,y\}}\sum_k|\widetilde Q_{g,c,e,r}(p_k)-Q_g(p_k)|.
\end{equation}
By the triangle inequality, $\overline L_{c,e}\leq2G_{c,e}$, but a small $L$ does not imply a small $G$. Adding the same error to both recovered groups yields zero contrast error while distorting both distributions. This is a mathematical limitation of the objective, not a decoder-specific anomaly.

A second control predicts zero difference at every quantile, with $L_0=(5s)^{-1}\sum_k|\Delta_k|$. We report $1-\overline L/L_0$ when $L_0>0$; negative values mean that decoding performs worse than this trivial prediction. When $L_0=0$, the relative skill is undefined and remains missing. Full group errors, zero-baseline losses and relative skills accompany every family result. Near-null contrasts can have high absolute scores even when the image adds little beyond predicting no difference.

A more basic limit occurs before rasterization: different distributions can have the same displayed summary. Under a common fixed axis, two mean-only displays may be identical despite different quantile targets. No decoder receiving the same image and metadata can distinguish those cases perfectly. The Supplement gives an explicit equal-mean, equal-variance construction and a lower bound. This motivates strong baselines that actually encode the declared target.

\subsection*{Sensitivity analyses accompany the selected score}
A reference optimum is conditional on its resolution, axes, computational observer and tuning budget. The supplementary audit varies these factors, reports near-optimal sets and minimax-regret choices, and distinguishes selection environments from a new display condition. Those historical expanded-candidate analyses retain their explicitly labeled nine- or ten-configuration scope; they are not silently relabeled as seven-family results. Source sensitivities omit flagged CKD values or unresolved-linkage retinopathy features without choosing corrections to improve scores. The 22 Parkinsons comparisons remain exploratory and excluded. No source data or underlying loss is changed in the familiar-candidate presentation.

\section*{Discussion}
The central result is a reproducible way to choose among familiar charts for a stated numerical task. Different datasets select different families under the same scoring rule, while some starting charts require no replacement. This is conditional optimization: the data, target, candidate set, tuning budget and observer jointly define the answer. Neither a diverse winner distribution nor a high score is sufficient to establish a generally useful recommender. The reference search necessarily matches or improves on any included starting candidate by construction; human benefit and performance beyond the selection conditions require separate evidence.

The main presentation deliberately restricts candidates to familiar families in response to the intended application. This restriction is retrospective and is not a discovery that specialized displays perform poorly. The supplementary direct-summary controls frequently perform better for this quantile-based objective. Keeping them visible prevents the familiar-set conclusion from being mistaken for unrestricted superiority. Conversely, the dominance of a task-matched control does not demonstrate that people find it easier to read. The figure examples illustrate computed outcomes without forcing a different winner for every dataset or every chart family.

The elementary eligibility criterion also separates questions that chart optimization cannot resolve. A marginal representation cannot recover a pairing-dependent target merely because its marginal quantiles are accurate. The same logic applies whenever distinct inputs collapse to the same encoded summary. An operational selector should reject an unsupported target and request an appropriate representation. It should then compare any admissible optimization with a strong default and report near-equivalent choices, failures and sensitivity rather than a uniquely authoritative rank.

These results do not establish that a 19-marker display is easier for a person to use than an interval or ECDF. It directly includes all summaries used in this benchmark, and a calibrated machine can exploit that alignment. Estimation effort, visual clutter, task switching and reader expertise are absent from the objective. Adding arbitrary penalties for those factors would not supply the missing evidence. A claim that optimization improves scientific communication requires those outcomes to be measured.

Decoder dependence remains a central limitation. The threshold observer and alpha observer use known calibration and family-specific recovery rules. Neither is independently trained or validated against human behavior. A probability heatmap's calibrated intensities are machine-readable without establishing perceptual accessibility.\cite{crameri2020} Opaque-dot overlap can impair an area-based decoder without implying that a different algorithm or person fails identically. Agreement between the two observers is therefore reassuring only within this narrow class of algorithms. Disagreement reveals fragility; it does not identify which observer better models a reader.

The benchmark also separates three kinds of uncertainty. Placement and resolution checks address rendering sensitivity. Resampling resource-level summaries describes variation among the nine convenience resources. Sampling uncertainty in population quantile differences is a different problem and is not estimated by either procedure. Participant-level source aggregation reduces repeated-measure inflation in HAR, BIDMC and wrist data, but features within a resource remain correlated. Small wrist cohorts, unresolved retinopathy linkage and questionable CKD values constrain biological interpretation. We study empirical image recovery, not disease effects inferred from these resources.

The zero-inclusive axis and SD normalization are deliberate but imperfect choices. Including zero can waste space for non-bar encodings; tight-axis results expose this dependence. Pooled SD is sensitive to extremes. Changing to another positive scale shared across candidates would rescale their losses without changing the ranking for that comparison; it could change pooled summaries across comparisons. Zero-scale fallback remains source-unit dependent. We disclose these limits rather than presenting a score threshold as a universal quality certificate.

Human validation remains the decisive next step for claims about readers. The included prospective protocol now treats selection versus a frozen task-matched default as its primary policy comparison and includes the dense control; it is a study plan, not completed evidence. A prospective experiment must freeze stimuli and computational predictions, use untouched cases, randomize display assignment, and measure estimation error alongside time and confidence. It should test whether the score adds predictive value beyond chart family and direct quantile baselines, accounting for repeated participants and cases. The accompanying protocol specifies these analyses and provides planning tools, but supplies no recruited participants, responses or approval claims. The present evidence supports computationally auditable choices for a fixed task; it does not establish improved human interpretation or clinical decisions.

\section*{Methods}
\subsection*{Source arrays and units of analysis}
The original prepared arrays are retained: WDBC 30 comparisons, Cleveland five, heart failure six, CKD 11, ILPD nine, retinopathy 16, HAR 40, BIDMC five and wrist five. HAR uses subject/activity medians from 30 participants. BIDMC summarizes readings within 46 source patients. Wrist exercise uses early/late ECG-reference summaries within eight participants.\cite{pimentel2017,jarchi2017} Missing observations and transformations remain recorded in the preparation pipeline. Paired resources are explicitly labeled, but the target compares marginal quantiles. Parkinsons contributes 22 exploratory comparisons only in the archived analysis.

\subsection*{Rendering and recovery}
The common reference interval includes zero and extends four times the larger group Scott bandwidth beyond the observed extremes, with a bandwidth floor of $0.01s$. Each group occupies a separate square panel of side 128, 256 or 512 pixels, with common measurement limits. Images are drawn at fourfold resolution, reduced with Lanczos filtering and blurred by a 0.5-pixel Gaussian. Original mark sizes and colors are unchanged. Four vertical placements are evaluated; raw-dot jitter also changes deterministically, whereas heatmap placements repeat the same strip. These are specified stress conditions, not independent random reader samples.

The original observer extracts thresholded colored or black pixels. The alternative projects RGB deviation from white onto the known group-color direction, clips the resulting alpha estimate to $[0,1]$, uses an alpha threshold of 0.2 for geometric masks and integrates alpha above 0.05 for area recovery. Heatmap recovery averages a local $3\times3$ neighborhood in the alternative observer. Both observers retain known layout and family-specific geometric assumptions. The Supplement and source code give exact rules and failure conditions.

The ECDF is a two-pixel step curve with cumulative probability on the horizontal axis and measurement on the vertical axis. Decoding estimates the ordinate at each target probability column. Finite-sample ECDF inversion differs from linear-interpolated empirical quantiles, a retained source of error. Quantile plots place radius-two-pixel markers at the five target probabilities; decoding uses local pixel-mass centroids, with no numerical target values in metadata. These baselines occupy the same panel area as the other charts. The six-task extension adds 19 markers at probabilities $.05,.10,\ldots,.95$, with the same radius and extraction window. Recovered quantiles are monotonized by cumulative maximum; consequently, a denser sequence can also change the recovered values at shared probabilities when adjacent estimates violate monotonicity. The extension evaluates 256- and 384-pixel panels with the placements specified above.

\subsection*{Selection and validation}
Loss is averaged across four placements before transformation. A failed placement invalidates the setting in that environment. Family summaries use the minimum valid loss within family. Exact ties use absolute tolerance $10^{-12}$; deterministic display order does not imply scientific superiority. The familiar-set equal-budget control uses the seven fixed settings in Table~\ref{tab:settings}; expanded controls are specified separately in the Supplement. The minimax rule requires a configuration to succeed in all six selection environments. The new-resolution transfer uses fixed selections and new placements without retuning.

Resource-disjoint selection requires a candidate to decode every training comparison, averages feature loss within each training resource and then weights resources equally. The selected configuration's held-out failures are retained. Descriptive bootstrap intervals for each family's loss difference from the quantile baseline resample resource means 5,000 times with a fixed seed; invalid comparisons are omitted and counts disclosed. These intervals concern the sampled resource summaries and are not population-generalization guarantees.

Both protocols are retrospective extensions, not preregistrations. In the earlier audit, a deterministic fixture failure motivated increasing the new ECDF stroke from one to two pixels before the final run; the stopped initial run supplies no reported results. Protocol, source and input hashes are recorded. The earlier checks cover all 23 settings and both observers, including blank-image rejection, pixel dependence, positive unit rescaling and metadata restrictions. Original reference losses are independently compared with the supplied archived CSVs. The six-task verification additionally recomputes every target from saved recovered quantiles, verifies resource exclusion for all 108 default-selection folds, and checks all 9,144 chart-policy transfer values. A zero-target control is evaluated separately and documented as a post-run diagnostic. Source code, per-trial recovered values, aggregate outputs and figure-generation scripts are included.
